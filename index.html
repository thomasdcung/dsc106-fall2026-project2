Proposition: At the NYPD, serious misconduct findings don't hurt an officer's career: the harsher the substantiated finding, the more likely the officer was promoted.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Grouping officers by their most serious outcome (score: -0.5)
    Each officer is placed by the harshest finding on their record, so someone with five “instructions” findings and one “charges” finding counts only as “charges.” This turns the question from how often an officer was disciplined into how badly, which makes the link to promotion sound more troubling. It's a defensible way to summarize a record, but we picked it because it gave the clearest climb. We considered summing severity points, but a single “worst outcome” was easier to explain on the chart.
  • Dashed baseline averaging the two mildest groups (score: -1)
    Combining instructions (34%) and training (30%) into one 32% line creates a low reference point that makes the charges bar look like a huge jump. It also hides that the trend isn't steady, and that training is actually lower than instructions. We explain what the line means in the top left, which keeps it from being a flat out trick, but we merged those two groups to sharpen the contrast.
  • “2.1x the rate” label inside the charges bar (score: -0.5)
    A ratio reads as more dramatic than the 33-point difference, and placing it in white text inside the darkest bar makes it the first thing readers see. The number is accurate (65% ÷ 32% ≈ 2.06), but it compares against our own constructed baseline rather than any single real group. Against instructions alone, it would only be 1.9x.
  • Sample sizes and a “how to read” note (score: +2)
    Each bar shows its n, and the footnote walks through one calculation (1,139 of 1,748 = 65%). Here the largest group is also the most damning one, so showing the sizes actually strengthens the argument: 65% is the most reliable number on the chart. This is a deliberate change from our first chart, where hiding n covered up a small group.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Using years since first complaint as a stand-in for time on the force (score: -0.5)
    The dataset has no hire date, so we used 2020 minus the year of the officer's first complaint. It is a weak proxy, because an officer who joined years earlier but got a first complaint late shows a shorter career than they have. It is also partly built in: an officer needs time to accumulate several allegations, so the bars are expected to rise. We labeled the axis “years since the officer's first complaint” and said in the footnote that it isn't a hire date. Even so, the chart could suggest more independence between complaints and tenure than the data supports.
  • Grouping by number of allegations instead of severity (score: -1)
    Visualization 4 groups officers by how many substantiated allegations they have, while Visualization 3 groups them by their most serious outcome, so the two charts do not share an axis. We chose allegation count because it produces a smooth staircase (10.6 to 14.2 years). Grouping by severity gives a messier pattern (about 8.8, 6.6, 10.1 and 14.2 years), where formalized training dips below instructions. The cost is that readers must assume that more allegations goes with more severe findings, and counting allegations instead of complaints stretches the high groups, since one incident can produce several allegations. We considered severity groups to match Visualization 3 but kept the cleaner chart.
  • A subtitle that links complaint history to promotion (score: -1)
    The title says officers with more allegations have longer complaint histories, which the chart shows. The subtitle adds that they therefore “had more time to be promoted,” which the chart doesn't show, since it has no promotion data. The causal chain (more time, more complaints, more promotion) is asserted, not demonstrated, and it leans on the reader accepting Visualization 3 as the support. We kept the claim because it is plausible and explains why the chart is on the “false” side, but it is the least supported sentence on the page.

Final Reflection

Building these two charts showed us that the hard part of persuasive visualization is not the code but deciding what to leave out. The mechanics were straightforward: group each officer by their worst substantiated outcome, count promotions, and draw bars. The difficult part was that the data can honestly tell both stories. The officers with the most serious findings really are promoted at the highest rate (65%), and those same officers also have much longer complaint histories, so the “for” chart is carried largely by career length. We were surprised how little deception the “for” chart needed. Every number is accurate, and the persuasion comes from choices that look innocent: a pooled baseline, a ratio label, a title that states a rule, and no mention of tenure inside the chart itself. The “against” chart ended up being the more honest one, because it works by adding context instead of removing it.

We now define ethical analysis as making sure a reader ends up believing what the data would show if they saw everything relevant to the question. A persuasive choice is acceptable when the numbers are accurate, the scope is stated (officers with at least one substantiated allegation, not “the NYPD”), the denominators are visible, and the claim stays inside what the data can support. A choice becomes misleading when it asks the reader to infer something the data does not show, such as treating a correlation as a cause, or when it hides the one thing that would change their conclusion. In our case that was tenure, and our “for” chart's title does exactly this. The bounds are fuzzy at the edges, since emphasis, framing and choice of baseline are unavoidable, so we judge a choice by whether a reader who learned about it afterward would feel misled.

If we did this again, we would let the caveat live in the same chart as the claim. Putting the tenure explanation in a separate “against” figure made the “for” chart more persuasive and less honest on its own, which is the trade-off this assignment is built to expose. The scores we gave ourselves reflect that: our most deceptive decisions (-1) were framing and grouping choices that make accurate numbers look cleaner or more dramatic than they are, and our most earnest one was showing sample sizes and defining every term in the caption.
